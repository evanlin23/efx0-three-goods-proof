\title{\efxz{} Allocations with at Most Three Relevant Goods}
\titlerunning{\efxz{} Allocations with at Most Three Relevant Goods}

\author{Evan Lin}
\authorrunning{E. Lin}
\institute{University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
An allocation of indivisible goods is \efxz{} if no agent envies another agent's bundle after removing any single good from it, even a good that the envious agent values at zero. We prove that every instance with nonnegative additive valuations in which each agent positively values at most three goods has a complete \efxz{} allocation in which all bundles but at most one have at most two goods. We also give an algorithm, Draft and Exchange (\DE{}), that computes such an allocation in time $O(n(n+m))$ with at most $4n$ trades. After an initial draft, goods are traded along a chain or a ring of agents, each of which gets a higher-ranked holding and loses no value, until a free agent can take the leftover goods, handing at most one to each other free agent (or every agent holds its pair). The main observation is that when no free agent can take them, every agent that does not yet hold its pair can point to an agent that would accept its good in a trade, and these pointers close a cycle: a ring, traversed against its arrows. The proof is machine-checked in Lean, as are the algorithm, its bound of $4n$ trades and the running time of an implementation.
\keywords{Fair division \and Indivisible goods \and EFX \and Trading cycles}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Let $N$ be a set of $n\ge1$ agents that share a set $M$ of $m$ indivisible goods and have nonnegative additive valuations $v_i(S)=\sum_{g\in S}v_i(g)$. An allocation $X$ assigns every good to exactly one agent, so no good is left out (allocations are \emph{complete}), and $X_i$ is the bundle of agent $i$. It is \emph{\efxz{}} if
\[
  v_i(X_i)\ \ge\ v_i(X_j\setminus\{h\})\qquad\text{for all } i\ne j \text{ and all } h\in X_j,
\]
while \emph{EFX} requires this only for goods $h$ with $v_i(h)>0$~\cite{AmanatidisBFV22}. Thus \efxz{} implies EFX, and the two notions coincide when all values are positive. Under \efxz{}, if $X_j$ contains a good that $i$ values at $0$, then $i$ must not envy $X_j$ at all. Whether complete EFX allocations always exist for additive valuations is open, although they exist in special cases~\cite{ChaudhuryGM20,HVGNV25,Mahara21,AlkassarFM26,Bratby26,AfshinmehrAMMS26,ChristodoulouFKS23,SgouritsaS25,KavianiSS24,LianeasSS26} (Table~\ref{tab:rw} in Appendix~\ref{app:rw}). Let $R_i=\{g\st v_i(g)>0\}$ be the set of \emph{relevant} goods of $i$. If $|R_i|\le2$ for all $i$, serial dictatorship gives an \efxz{} allocation~\cite{MrdEfx}. With three goods this fails: an agent that values $a$, $b$, $c$ at $4$, $3$, $2$ may take $a$ and later find $b$ and $c$ in the last agent's bundle with a third good, and removing that third good leaves $b$ and $c$, worth $5>4$.

\begin{theorem}[existence and shape]\label{thm:main}
Every instance with $n\ge1$ agents and nonnegative additive valuations in which every agent positively values at most three goods has a complete \efxz{} allocation in which all bundles but at most one have at most two goods.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[algorithm]\label{thm:algo}
Algorithm \DE{} (Section~\ref{sec:algo}) computes such an allocation with at most $n$ peeling rounds and at most $4n$ trades, in $O(n(n+m))$ steps of a unit-cost random-access machine. Every decision of \DE{} compares two sums of at most two values of one agent, and each such comparison counts as one step.
\end{theorem}

\paragraph{What is new.} As far as we know,\footnote{Literature search of 25 September 2026 (arXiv, the web, Zenodo, Google Scholar, Crossref, the EC~2026 programme), recorded in \file{proofs/novelty.md} of~\cite{Repo}.} no earlier result settles the case of three goods. Viswanathan and Mehta~\cite{ViswanathanM24} study the same kind of restriction, with up to four relevant goods per agent, but for ordinary EFX and with only a proof sketch. Their result does not imply ours, because EFX does not imply \efxz{}, and the difference matters precisely when agents value few goods, so that most goods are worth $0$ to most agents. In the example above, suppose the agent holds $a$, worth $4$, and another bundle is $\{b,c,g\}$ with $v(g)=0$. Removing $b$ or $c$ leaves $2$ or $3$, so EFX holds, but removing $g$ leaves $5>4$, so \efxz{} fails. Their algorithm gives the goods that nobody values to an arbitrary agent, which can create such a bundle. None of the existence results for complete \efxz{} allocations surveyed in Table~\ref{tab:rw} covers all instances with at most three relevant goods per agent. Apart from~\cite{MrdEfx}, which allows at most two relevant goods per agent, each bounds a quantity that our model leaves free: the number of agents, the number of goods compared with agents, the number of agents valuing a good, the girth of the hypergraph of goods, or the number of distinct valuations. Appendix~\ref{app:rw} gives an instance with six agents that none of them covers and checks it row by row. Our contributions are the existence result (Theorem~\ref{thm:main}), the algorithm (Theorem~\ref{thm:algo}), a machine-checked proof (Appendix~\ref{sec:evidence} lists what is checked), and the shape of the allocation, which has no counterpart we know of and cannot be strengthened in two natural ways (Appendix~\ref{app:limits}). Some instances have no \efxz{} allocation with all bundles of at most two goods, and on an instance with four agents every \efxz{} allocation has bundle sizes $4,1,1,1$, so the large bundle cannot be limited to three goods.

\paragraph{How the proof works.} Agents that can leave with at most one good do so, and the others draft at most one good each. Then, until a \emph{free} agent can take the leftover goods, handing at most one to each other free agent (or every agent holds its pair), goods are traded along a \emph{chain} or a \emph{ring} of agents, each of which gets a higher-ranked holding (its pair ranks above its top) and loses no value. Progress is measured by the total score, the sum of these ranks, and not by welfare, because an agent moving between two goods of equal value gains score but no value. Section~\ref{sec:algo} gives a small example.

\section{Peeling}\label{sec:peel}

The proofs of Sections~\ref{sec:peel} and~\ref{sec:states} are in Appendix~\ref{app:proofs}.

Agents and goods are numbered, and ``index order'' and ``the first'' refer to these numbers. A good is \emph{alone} in an allocation if it is the only good of its bundle.

\begin{lemma}[peeling]\label{lem:peel}
Let $i\in N$ and $P\subseteq M$ with (i) $v_i(P)\ge v_i(M\setminus P)$ and (ii) $|P|\le1$. If the instance with agents $N\setminus\{i\}$ and goods $M\setminus P$ has an \efxz{} allocation $Z$, then $Z$ together with $X_i=P$ is an \efxz{} allocation of the whole instance.
\end{lemma}
\noindent \emph{Rule R1.} Let $G$ be the set of remaining goods, and let $p$ be agent $i$'s favourite good in $G$, the one of largest value (the lower index breaks ties). If $G=\emptyset$ or $v_i(p)=0$, so that $i$ values no remaining good, then $i$ may leave with $P=\emptyset$. If $v_i(p)>0$ and $v_i(G\setminus\{p\})\le v_i(p)$, then $i$ may leave with $P=\{p\}$. Rule R1 applies, for instance, to every agent that values at most two remaining goods. Hence, if every agent values at most two goods, serial dictatorship is \efxz{}~\cite{MrdEfx}.

\noindent When $|R_i|=3$ we name agent $i$'s relevant goods $a_i$, $b_i$, $c_i$ so that $v_i(a_i)\ge v_i(b_i)\ge v_i(c_i)>0$, breaking ties in favour of the lower index. This gives a strict \emph{ranking} $a_i\succ_i b_i\succ_i c_i$, and $g\succ_i h$ implies $v_i(g)\ge v_i(h)$. Agent $i$ is \emph{strictly balanced} if $v_i(a_i)<v_i(b_i)+v_i(c_i)$.

\begin{lemma}[when nobody can leave]\label{lem:R1fail}
Let agent $i$ value at most three goods of the set $G$ of remaining goods, and suppose that rule R1 does not apply to $i$. Then $i$ values exactly three goods of $G$ and is strictly balanced with respect to them.
\end{lemma}
\noindent Throughout Sections~\ref{sec:states}--\ref{sec:improve} we work in the \emph{core case}, in which every agent values exactly three goods and is strictly balanced; there may also be goods that no agent values.

\section{States}\label{sec:states}

\begin{definition}[states and scores]\label{def:state}
A \emph{state} $Y$ gives each agent $i$ a \emph{holding} $Y_i$: nothing, $\{a_i\}$, $\{b_i\}$, $\{c_i\}$, or its \emph{pair} $\{b_i,c_i\}$, with the holdings pairwise disjoint. $U$ is the set of agents holding their pair. The goods held by nobody are \emph{leftover}; $J$ is the set of leftover goods. Agent $i$'s \emph{score} is $4$, $3$, $2$, $1$ or $0$ if it holds its pair, $a_i$, $b_i$, $c_i$ or nothing. Since $v_i(a_i)<v_i(b_i)+v_i(c_i)$, a higher score never means a lower value.
\end{definition}

\noindent A higher score need not mean a higher value, however: if $v_i(a_i)=v_i(b_i)$, moving from $b_i$ to $a_i$ raises the score but not the value. Gaining the pair, or gaining a good after holding nothing, does raise the value. The trades below raise scores, and the total score $\Phi(Y)=\sum_i\mathrm{score}_i(Y)$, an integer between $0$ and $4n$, measures progress.

\begin{definition}[wants, validity, free agents]\label{def:valid}
Let $Y$ be a state. An agent $i\notin U$ \emph{wants} a good $g\in R_i$ if it holds nothing, or holds a good $y$ with $g\succ_i y$. Agents in $U$ want nothing. $Y$ is \emph{valid} if every good that some agent wants is held alone: it is the whole holding of some agent. An agent $i\notin U$ is \emph{free} if it holds nothing or holds a good that nobody wants. $F$ is the set of free agents.
\end{definition}

\noindent No agent wants its own good, so the holder of a wanted good is some other agent; in a valid state, this holder is neither in $U$ nor free. Leftover goods and the goods of pair holders are not wanted in a valid state, and an agent outside $U\cup F$ holds a single good that some agent wants.

Validity is what makes finishing possible. As it keeps every wanted good alone, a bundle of two or more goods can contain only goods that nobody wants. An agent holding nothing wants all its goods, and an agent holding a single good wants every good it ranks higher. Such a bundle therefore contains none of the relevant goods of an agent holding nothing, and, of the relevant goods of an agent holding a single good, only goods worth at most that good. One obstruction remains, which arises only with three goods: an agent holding its top $a_x$ may find $b_x$ and $c_x$ together in a bundle with a third good, while $v_x(b_x)+v_x(c_x)>v_x(a_x)$. Section~\ref{sec:finish} deals with this case.

\begin{lemma}[the draft]\label{lem:draft}
Let the agents, in any order, each take the first of $a_i$, $b_i$, $c_i$ not yet taken, or nothing if all three are taken. The result is a valid state with $U=\emptyset$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{lemma}[staying valid]\label{lem:stay}
Let $Y$ be valid, and $Y'$ a state in which every agent's score is at least its score in $Y$ and every good wanted in $Y$ is held alone. Then $Y'$ is valid.
\end{lemma}

\section{Finishing}\label{sec:finish}

\begin{definition}[blockers, finishing]\label{def:finish}
Let $Y$ be a state and $o$ a free agent. A \emph{blocker} of $o$ is an agent $x\ne o$ that holds exactly its top, $Y_x=\{a_x\}$, and has $b_x,c_x\in J\cup Y_o$. Agent $o$ \emph{can finish} with a set $H\subseteq J$ if $|H|\le|F|-1$ and $H$ contains $b_x$ or $c_x$ for every blocker $x$ of $o$. Its \emph{completion} $X$ gives each good of $H$ to a different free agent other than $o$, gives $o$ the bundle $X_o=Y_o\cup(J\setminus H)$, and lets every other agent keep its holding (plus its good of $H$, if any).
\end{definition}

\noindent Informally, a blocker $x$ of $o$ would find both $b_x$ and $c_x$ in $o$'s bundle if $o$ took all the leftover goods, and the set $H$ moves one of the two out of that bundle. When every agent holds its pair, the completion with any agent $o$ gives $o$ the bundle $Y_o\cup J$ and lets the others keep their pairs.

\begin{theorem}[soundness]\label{thm:sound}
Let $Y$ be a valid state, and $X$ the completion with a free agent $o$ that can finish with a set $H$, or, if every agent holds its pair, with any agent $o$. Then $X$ is an \efxz{} allocation, and every bundle other than $X_o$ has at most two goods.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Every bundle other than $X_o$ has at most two goods: a free agent other than $o$ holds at most one good and receives at most one good of $H$, a pair holder holds two goods, and every other agent keeps a single good.

Wanted goods stay alone: if $g$ is wanted and held alone by $j$, then $j$ is not free, so $j\ne o$, $j$ receives no good of $H$, and $X_j=\{g\}$. Hence every good in a bundle of two or more goods is unwanted.

It remains to rule out envy. Let $i\ne j$ and $h\in X_j$; we show that $v_i(X_j\setminus\{h\})\le v_i(X_i)$, where $v_i(X_i)\ge v_i(Y_i)$. If $|X_j|\le1$, then $X_j\setminus\{h\}=\emptyset$. So let $|X_j|\ge2$, and let $T=X_j\cap R_i$; it contains no good that $i$ wants and no good of $i$'s holding.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $i$ holds its pair, then $T\subseteq\{a_i\}$, and $v_i(T)\le v_i(a_i)<v_i(b_i)+v_i(c_i)$.
\item If $i$ holds nothing, it wants all of $R_i$, so $T=\emptyset$.
\item If $Y_i=\{y\}$, every good of $T$ is ranked below $y$ and worth at most $v_i(y)$. If $|T|\le1$, then $v_i(X_j\setminus\{h\})\le v_i(T)\le v_i(y)$. If $|T|=2$, then $y=a_i$ and $T=\{b_i,c_i\}$. If $X_j=\{b_i,c_i\}$, removing $h$ leaves one good, worth at most $v_i(b_i)\le v_i(a_i)$. Otherwise $|X_j|\ge3$, so $j=o$ by the first step; since $b_i,c_i\in X_o\subseteq Y_o\cup J$, agent $i$ is a blocker of $o$. Then $H$ contains $b_i$ or $c_i$, which went to an agent other than $o$, a contradiction. (When every agent holds its pair, only the first case occurs.) \qed
\end{itemize}
\end{proof}

\section{Improving}\label{sec:improve}

\begin{definition}[rings]\label{def:ring}
Let $Y$ be a state. A \emph{ring} is a cycle $w_0\to w_1\to\dots\to w_{k-1}\to w_0$ of $k\ge2$ distinct agents outside $U$, each holding a single good $y_t=y_{w_t}$, such that each arrow $w_t\to w_{t+1}$ (indices modulo $k$) is
\begin{itemize}
\item a \emph{want arrow}: $w_t$ wants $y_{t+1}$; or
\item a \emph{pair arrow}: $w_{t+1}$ is free, $w_t$ is a blocker of $w_{t+1}$, and the pair of $w_t$ is $\{y_{t+1},h_t\}$ with $h_t\in J$;
\end{itemize}
and the leftover goods $h_t$ of the pair arrows are pairwise distinct. \emph{Trading along the ring} gives each $w_t$ the good $y_{t+1}$, together with $h_t$ for a pair arrow, in place of its own good; agents off the ring keep their holdings.
\end{definition}

\noindent As in envy graphs, an arrow points from an agent to the holder of a good it wants, or from a blocker to the free agent whose good completes its pair. Goods move against the arrows: along $w_t\to w_{t+1}$, the good of $w_{t+1}$ goes to $w_t$. In a valid state, an arrow into a free agent is a pair arrow, because nobody wants its good, and an arrow into any other agent is a want arrow.

\begin{lemma}[ring]\label{lem:ring}
Trading along a ring of a valid state gives a valid state in which every agent of the ring has a higher score and every other agent the same score.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The result is a state: each good $y_{t+1}$ moves to $w_t$ only, the goods $h_t$ are distinct leftover goods, and agents off the ring keep goods that are neither. Each new holding is a single good of its receiver or the receiver's pair. Scores rise along the ring, since along a want arrow $w_t$ receives a good it ranks above what it held, and along a pair arrow $w_t$ goes from its top to its pair. Finally, let $g$ be wanted in $Y$, held alone by $j$. If $j$ is off the ring, it keeps $g$. If $j=w_{t+1}$, then $w_{t+1}$ is not free, so the arrow into it is a want arrow, and $w_t$ now holds $g$ alone. Lemma~\ref{lem:stay} gives validity. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[chain]\label{lem:chain}
Let $Y$ be valid, and $x$ an agent with $Y_x=\{a_x\}$ and $b_x,c_x\in J$. Let $j_0=x$, and as long as $j_t$ holds a good that some agent wants, let $j_{t+1}$ be the first such agent. If an agent repeats, the agents from its first occurrence on, taken in reverse order, form a ring of want arrows. Otherwise the sequence stops at an agent $j_k$ ($k\ge0$) that holds nothing or a good nobody wants, and the following gives a valid state in which $x,j_1,\dots,j_k$ have higher scores and every other agent the same score: $x$ takes its pair; each $j_t$ with $1\le t\le k$ takes the good of $j_{t-1}$; the old good of $j_k$, if any, becomes leftover.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Agent $x$ holds its top and wants nothing, so $j_t\ne x$ for $t\ge1$. If $j_s=j_t$ with $1\le s<t$ is the first repeat, then $j_s,\dots,j_{t-1}$ are distinct agents outside $U$, each holding a good that the next one wants (and $j_s=j_t$ wants the good of $j_{t-1}$), so $j_{t-1}\to j_{t-2}\to\dots\to j_s\to j_{t-1}$ is a ring of want arrows ($t-s\ge2$, as no agent wants its own good). Otherwise the result is a state, since $b_x$, $c_x$ were leftover and each good that moves goes one step. Agent $x$ goes from its top to its pair, and each $j_t$, $t\ge1$, receives a good it wants. A wanted good is neither $b_x$ nor $c_x$, which are leftover. If its holder is off the path, it keeps the good; if its holder is $j_t$, then $t<k$ (the good of $j_k$ is not wanted), and $j_{t+1}$ now holds it alone. Lemma~\ref{lem:stay} gives validity. \qed
\end{proof}

\noindent Applying a chain or trading along a ring is called a \emph{trade}. By Lemmas~\ref{lem:ring} and~\ref{lem:chain}, a trade keeps the state valid and raises the total score $\Phi$ by at least one. From now on we assume that no chain applies:

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{(NC)} no agent $x$ with $Y_x=\{a_x\}$ has both $b_x$ and $c_x$ in $J$.
\smallskip

\begin{lemma}[the finishing test]\label{lem:test}
Let $Y$ be a state with (NC), and $o$ a free agent.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] If $o$ holds nothing, it has no blocker.
\item[(b)] If $x$ is a blocker of $o$, then $o$ holds a good $y_o$, and the pair of $x$ is $\{y_o,h_x\}$ for a leftover good $h_x$.
\item[(c)] Let $H_o$ be the set of the goods $h_x$ of the blockers $x$ of $o$. Then $o$ can finish if and only if $|H_o|\le|F|-1$, and then it can finish with $H_o$. In particular a free agent holding nothing can finish.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $x$ be a blocker of $o$. Then $b_x,c_x\in J\cup Y_o$, and by (NC) not both are in $J$, so one of them is in $Y_o$, which proves (a). As $o$ holds at most one good, that good is $y_o$, and the other one, $h_x$, is leftover, which proves (b). A set $H\subseteq J$ contains $b_x$ or $c_x$ if and only if it contains $h_x$, so the sets $H$ allowed by Definition~\ref{def:finish} are those with $H_o\subseteq H\subseteq J$ and $|H|\le|F|-1$, which exist if and only if $|H_o|\le|F|-1$. If $o$ holds nothing, then $H_o=\emptyset$ and $|F|\ge1$. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Improvement Lemma]\label{thm:improve}
Let $Y$ be a valid state in which not every agent holds its pair and no free agent can finish. Then a chain or a ring gives a valid state in which every agent has at least its score and some agent a higher one.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
If (NC) fails, Lemma~\ref{lem:chain} applies, so assume (NC). By Lemma~\ref{lem:test}, every free agent $o$ then holds a good and has $|H_o|\ge|F|$.

We first choose distinct leftover goods. List the free agents in index order as $o_1,\dots,o_f$. For $i=1,\dots,f$ in turn, let $x_{o_i}$ be the first blocker of $o_i$ whose leftover good is not among $q_{o_1},\dots,q_{o_{i-1}}$, and let $q_{o_i}=h_{x_{o_i}}$. This blocker exists because the blockers of $o_i$ have $|H_{o_i}|\ge f>i-1$ different leftover goods.

Next, for every agent $j\notin U$ let $\sigma(j)=x_j$ if $j$ is free; otherwise let $\sigma(j)$ be the first agent that wants the good of $j$, which exists because $j\notin U\cup F$. In both cases $\sigma(j)\notin U$, $\sigma(j)\ne j$, and $\sigma(j)\to j$ is an arrow: a pair arrow if $j$ is free, and a want arrow otherwise. So $\sigma(j)$ is the agent that would receive the good of $j$, and following $\sigma$ follows the goods, against the arrows.

To find the ring, start at the first agent outside $U$ and follow $\sigma$ until an agent repeats. The agents from its first occurrence on, taken in reverse order, form a cycle $w_0\to\dots\to w_{k-1}\to w_0$ with $\sigma(w_{t+1})=w_t$ and $k\ge2$, each holding a single good (for free agents, by Lemma~\ref{lem:test}). By Lemma~\ref{lem:test}(b), the arrow into a free agent $w_{t+1}$ is a pair arrow with $h_t=q_{w_{t+1}}$, and these goods are distinct for distinct free agents. Every other arrow is a want arrow. Hence the cycle is a ring, and Lemma~\ref{lem:ring} applies. \qed
\end{proof}

\section{The Algorithm Draft and Exchange}\label{sec:algo}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:de} puts these pieces together; ties are broken by index as in Section~\ref{sec:peel}.

\begin{algorithm}[!b]
\caption{Draft and Exchange (\DE{})}\label{alg:de}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require agents $N$, goods $M$, nonnegative values $v_i(g)$, every agent with $|R_i|\le3$
\State $A\gets N$, $G\gets M$
\While{$|A|\ge2$ and rule R1 applies to an agent of $A$, for $G$} \Comment{peeling}
  \State $i\gets$ the first such agent; $X_i\gets P_i$ (by rule R1); $A\gets A\setminus\{i\}$, $G\gets G\setminus P_i$
\EndWhile
\If{$|A|=1$} give $G$ to the agent of $A$ and \Return $X$ \EndIf
\For{$i\in A$ in index order} \Comment{the draft}
  \State $Y_i\gets$ the first of $\{a_i\},\{b_i\},\{c_i\}$ whose good is not taken, else nothing
\EndFor
\Loop
  \If{some $x$ with $Y_x=\{a_x\}$ has $b_x,c_x\in J$} \Comment{chain}
    \State apply the chain of the first such $x$, or the ring of want arrows it runs into (Lemma~\ref{lem:chain})
  \ElsIf{every agent holds its pair} \Comment{finish}
    \State $o\gets$ the first agent; $H\gets\emptyset$; \textbf{break}
  \ElsIf{some free agent $o$ has $|H_o|\le|F|-1$} \Comment{finish}
    \State $o\gets$ the first such agent; $H\gets H_o$; \textbf{break}
  \Else \Comment{ring}
    \State trade along the ring of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:improve}
  \EndIf
\EndLoop
\State give the $k$-th good of $H$ (in the order of first blockers) to the $k$-th free agent other than $o$
\State $X_o\gets Y_o\cup(J\setminus H)$; every other $i\in A$ keeps $Y_i$ (plus its good of $H$, if any)
\State \Return $X$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{theorem}[\DE{} is correct]\label{thm:de}
On every instance in which every agent values at most three goods, \DE{} returns a complete \efxz{} allocation in which all bundles but at most one have at most two goods. It peels at most $n$ agents and makes at most $4n$ trades.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each peeling round removes one agent, so there are at most $n$ rounds. For the agent $i$ peeled with $P_i$, rule R1 amounts to conditions (i) and (ii) of Lemma~\ref{lem:peel} on the instance left before its round, so by induction from the last round down it suffices that \DE{} returns an \efxz{} allocation of the agents $A$ and goods $G$ left at the end. If $|A|=1$, the single agent has no other bundle to envy.

Now let $|A|\ge2$. Rule R1 applies to no agent of $A$, so by Lemma~\ref{lem:R1fail} we are in the core case. The draft gives a valid state (Lemma~\ref{lem:draft}). A chain or a ring gives a valid state in which no score drops and some score rises (Lemmas~\ref{lem:chain} and~\ref{lem:ring}); by the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:improve}, the ring exists whenever it is used. The total score $\Phi$ is an integer between $0$ and $4|A|$ and rises by at least one with every trade, so there are at most $4|A|\le4n$ trades, and the loop stops. When it stops, either every agent holds its pair or $o$ can finish with $H$ (by Lemma~\ref{lem:test}(c), since the failed chain test gives (NC)), and Theorem~\ref{thm:sound} gives an \efxz{} allocation in which only $X_o$ can have more than two goods. Peeled agents receive at most one good each. \qed
\end{proof}

\paragraph{A small example.} Agents $z$, $x$, $o$, in index order, rank $g_4\succ g_0\succ g_1$, $g_0\succ g_1\succ g_2$ and $g_0\succ g_4\succ g_1$, respectively, with values $4$, $3$, $2$; nobody values $g_3$. No agent is peeled. In the draft $z$ takes $g_4$, $x$ takes $g_0$ and $o$ takes $g_1$, so the leftover goods are $g_2$ and $g_3$. Agent $o$ wants $g_0$ and $g_4$, which are held alone, so the state is valid, and $o$ is the only free agent. No chain applies. Agent $x$ is a blocker of $o$: if $o$ took $\{g_1,g_2,g_3\}$, removing $g_3$ would leave $g_1$ and $g_2$, worth $5>4$ to $x$. Thus $H_o=\{g_2\}$, and as no other free agent can take $g_2$, $o$ cannot finish. The pointers are $\sigma(o)=x$, for the pair arrow $x\to o$ ($x$'s pair is $o$'s good $g_1$ and the leftover $g_2$), and $\sigma(x)=\sigma(z)=o$, for the want arrows $o\to x$ and $o\to z$ ($o$ wants $g_0$ and $g_4$). Following $\sigma$ from $z$ reaches $o$ and then $x$, and $\sigma(x)=o$ again: the ring is $x\to o\to x$, and along it $x$ takes $\{g_1,g_2\}$ and $o$ takes $g_0$. Now nobody wants anything, the free agents are $z$ and $o$, and $z$ has no blocker, so $z$ finishes, and \DE{} returns the \efxz{} allocation $X_z=\{g_4,g_3\}$, $X_x=\{g_1,g_2\}$, $X_o=\{g_0\}$.

\paragraph{Running time.} We count steps of a unit-cost random-access machine, in which an array access, an arithmetic operation on indices, and a comparison of two sums of at most two values of one agent each take one step. This is not a bound on bit complexity for arbitrary encodings of the values. \DE{} keeps an array of the holder of each good (or a mark that the good is leftover or peeled) and an array of the holding of each agent.
One pass over the $nm$ values finds the at most three relevant goods of each agent, and sorting them gives its ranking, in $O(nm)$ steps. To test rule R1 for agent $i$, we look only at its relevant goods still in $G$, find its favourite $p$ and compare $v_i(p)$ with the sum of the at most two other values; a peeling round thus tests every agent in $O(n)$ steps and removes at most one good.

An iteration of the loop takes $O(n+m)$ steps. Every agent outside $U$ marks the at most three goods it wants, and each good records the first agent, in index order, that wants it. Then one pass finds the leftover goods, another the free agents, and a third runs the chain test, which looks up the holders of $b_x$ and $c_x$ for every $x$ holding $a_x$. A chain follows the recorded agents, marking those it visits, in $O(n)$ steps. Under (NC), an agent $x$ holding $a_x$ is a blocker of $o$ exactly when one of $b_x$, $c_x$ is leftover and the other is the good of the free agent $o$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:test}). Each agent is therefore a blocker of at most one free agent, and one pass in index order lists the blockers of every free agent, each list in index order. One pass over these lists, with marks on goods, gives the sizes $|H_o|$, and another gives the distinct leftover goods $q_o$ of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:improve}. Then each $\sigma(j)$ takes one step, and following $\sigma$ while marking visited agents finds the ring in $O(n)$ steps. A trade and the final completion each take $O(n+m)$ steps. With at most $n$ peeling rounds and at most $4n+1$ loop iterations (the last one finishes), \DE{} takes $O(nm+n^2+n(n+m))=O(n(n+m))$ steps. For a counted program, Lean proves a bound of $750(n+1)(n+m+1)$ steps (Appendix~\ref{sec:evidence}).

\paragraph{Real values.} Every decision of \DE{} compares one value of an agent with $0$, with another of its values, or with the sum of two of its values, and the proofs use nothing else about the values. Theorem~\ref{thm:de} and the remarks above therefore prove Theorems~\ref{thm:main} and~\ref{thm:algo}, for real values as well. Appendix~\ref{sec:evidence} explains how the Lean proof handles real values.

\section{Further Results, Verification and Open Problems}\label{sec:concl}

Appendix~\ref{sec:example} has a larger example, in which \DE{}'s ring gives pairs to two agents at once; rings may need any number of pair arrows (Appendix~\ref{app:size}), and some instances need a bundle of three or four goods (Appendix~\ref{app:limits}). Every result here is machine-checked in core Lean~4 (\file{lean/EFX/K3DE*.lean}, with \file{RealValues.lean} and \file{K3Real.lean} for real values, in~\cite{Repo}), including the main theorem for natural-number values, its extension to real values through natural numbers that answer the same comparisons, and the running time of a counted program that returns \DE{}'s output. Appendix~\ref{sec:evidence} maps each result to its Lean theorem (Table~\ref{tab:lean}) and says what is not formalized. Tests found no failure. All reviews so far were done by AI agents, and there has been no human peer review yet. Three questions remain open. Does every instance in which each agent positively values at most four goods have a complete \efxz{} allocation? (For ordinary EFX, Viswanathan and Mehta~\cite{ViswanathanM24} announce a positive answer.) Do fewer than $4n$ trades suffice? What is the least bound on the size of the large bundle that holds for every instance?

\section{Proofs of Sections~\ref{sec:peel} and~\ref{sec:states}, and a Corollary}\label{app:proofs}

\begin{proof}[of Lemma~\ref{lem:peel}]
For agent $i$ and a bundle $Z_j$, $v_i(Z_j\setminus\{h\})\le v_i(Z_j)\le v_i(M\setminus P)\le v_i(P)$. For an agent $j\ne i$ and the bundle $P$, removing its only good leaves nothing, and for $j\ne i$ and a bundle $Z_k$, the condition holds because $Z$ is \efxz{}. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[of Lemma~\ref{lem:R1fail}]
Since rule R1 does not apply, $v_i(p)>0$ and $v_i(G\setminus\{p\})>v_i(p)$. If $i$ valued at most two goods of $G$, then $v_i(G\setminus\{p\})$ would be the value of at most one good, and hence at most $v_i(p)$. Therefore $i$ values exactly three goods of $G$; $p$ is its top, and $v_i(b_i)+v_i(c_i)>v_i(a_i)$. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[of Lemma~\ref{lem:draft}]
Each agent holds one of its goods or nothing, and no good is taken twice. If $i$ wants $g$, then $g$ was taken before $i$'s turn by an earlier agent, as that agent's only good, and holdings never change during the draft. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[of Lemma~\ref{lem:stay}]
Let $i$ want $g$ in $Y'$. If $i$ holds nothing in $Y'$, its score in $Y$ was $0$, so it held nothing in $Y$ and wanted $g$ there too. If $i$ holds a single good $y'$ in $Y'$, its score in $Y$ was below $4$, so in $Y$ it held either nothing or a good $y$ with $y'=y$ or $y'\succ_i y$; since $g\succ_i y'$, again $i$ wanted $g$ in $Y$. Hence $g$ is held alone in $Y'$. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[score-Pareto-maximal valid states]\label{cor:po}
In the core case, in every valid state that no valid state Pareto-dominates in scores, either every agent holds its pair or some free agent can finish. In particular every core instance has a complete \efxz{} allocation in which all bundles but at most one have at most two goods.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The first part follows from Theorem~\ref{thm:improve}. There are valid states (Lemma~\ref{lem:draft}), and only finitely many, so one with the largest total score is not Pareto-dominated in scores, and Theorem~\ref{thm:sound} applies to it. \qed
\end{proof}

\noindent This gives a second existence argument, within the valid states, but not an efficiency guarantee: maximality is in scores and among valid states only, and we claim nothing about the efficiency of the resulting allocation or of \DE{}'s output.

\paragraph{The draft.} No agent is peeled. The four $x$'s take their tops, $o_1$ finds $g_2$ and $g_3$ taken and takes $g_4$, and $o_2$ likewise takes $g_5$. Agent $o_1$ wants $g_2$ and $g_3$, agent $o_2$ wants $g_0$ and $g_1$, and the $x$'s want nothing; all four wanted goods are held alone. The leftover goods are $g_6,\dots,g_9$, and the free agents are $o_1$ and $o_2$. No chain applies, since the $b$ of each $x$ is held by $o_1$ or $o_2$.

\paragraph{Nobody can finish.} The blockers of $o_1$ are $x_1$, with pair $\{g_4,g_6\}$, and $x_1'$, with pair $\{g_4,g_7\}$, so $H_{o_1}=\{g_6,g_7\}$; symmetrically, $H_{o_2}=\{g_8,g_9\}$. With $H=\{g_6\}$, agent $o_1$ gets $X_{o_1}=\{g_4,g_7,g_8,g_9\}$, and $x_1'$, holding $g_1$ (worth $4$), values $X_{o_1}$ without $g_8$ at $3+2=5$. In fact exactly four valid states Pareto-dominate the draft state in scores, and each gives pairs to two agents.

\section{How Large Must a Ring Be?}\label{app:size}

A \emph{short move} is a chain, or a ring with at most one pair arrow. A natural, simpler algorithm would use only short moves. The next proposition shows that, among states in which short moves are not enough, the instance of Appendix~\ref{sec:example} has the smallest possible number of agents.

\begin{proposition}[when short moves are not enough]\label{prop:short}
In the core case, let $Y$ be a valid state in which not every agent holds its pair, no free agent can finish, and no short move applies. Then $|F|\ge2$, $n\ge6$ and $m\ge8$; if $|F|\ge3$, then $n\ge9$ and $m\ge12$. The bounds $n=6$ and $m=8$ are attained.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
No chain applies, so (NC) holds, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:test} every free agent $o$ holds a good and has $|H_o|\ge|F|$, hence at least $|F|$ blockers. The blockers of two different free agents $o,o'$ are different, since a common blocker would have the pair $\{y_o,y_{o'}\}$, with no leftover good, contrary to Lemma~\ref{lem:test}(b). There is no ring of want arrows, so a walk that repeatedly moves to an agent that wants the current agent's good, backwards along a want arrow, never repeats an agent; it ends at an agent that holds nothing or a good nobody wants, that is, at a free agent. Every agent holds a good: pair holders hold two, free agents one by Lemma~\ref{lem:test}, and the others a wanted good. Let $k=|F|$; then $k\ge1$, because the walk from an agent outside $U$ ends at a free agent.

If $k=1$, let $F=\{o\}$ and let $x$ be a blocker of $o$. Then $x$ is not free, so its walk ends at $o$. The want arrows of the walk lead back from $o$ to $x$, and together with the pair arrow $x\to o$ they form a ring with one pair arrow, which is a short move. Hence $k\ge2$.

If $k=2$, let $F=\{o_1,o_2\}$. Agent $o_1$ has at least two blockers and $o_2$ is the only other free agent, so some blocker $x$ of $o_1$ is not free. As above, its walk cannot end at $o_1$, so it ends at $o_2$, and its last step goes backwards along a want arrow from $o_2$ ($x\ne o_2$). Agent $o_2$ therefore wants a good and does not hold its top, so $o_2$ is not a blocker of $o_1$, and no blocker of $o_1$ is free. The same holds for $o_2$. Hence $n\ge2+2+2=6$ and $m\ge n+|J|\ge6+2=8$.

If $k\ge3$, the blockers of the $k$ free agents include at least $k^2$ different agents, at most $k$ of which are free, so $n\ge|F|+(k^2-k)=k^2\ge9$, and $m\ge n+|J|\ge k^2+k\ge12$.

The instance of Appendix~\ref{sec:example} attains $n=6$ (with $m=10$). For $m=8$, let $o_1$ rank $g_4\succ g_5\succ g_0$, $o_2$ rank $g_6\succ g_7\succ g_1$, $x_1$ rank $g_4\succ g_1\succ g_2$, $x_1'$ rank $g_5\succ g_3\succ g_1$, $x_2$ rank $g_6\succ g_0\succ g_2$ and $x_2'$ rank $g_7\succ g_3\succ g_0$, and let $o_1$ hold $g_0$, $o_2$ hold $g_1$ and every $x$ its top. Then $J=\{g_2,g_3\}$ and $F=\{o_1,o_2\}$; the blockers of $o_2$ are $x_1$, $x_1'$ (leftover goods $g_2$, $g_3$) and those of $o_1$ are $x_2$, $x_2'$ (leftover goods $g_2$, $g_3$). Nobody can finish, and no short move applies. \qed
\end{proof}

\noindent In the second instance, distinctness of the leftover goods matters. Of the four cycles $o_2\to x'\to o_1\to x\to o_2$ with two pair arrows ($x\in\{x_1,x_1'\}$, $x'\in\{x_2,x_2'\}$), two use the same leftover good twice, and trading along them would give that good to two agents; only the other two are rings.

\paragraph{No bounded number of pair arrows suffices.} For every $k\ge2$ and $d\ge1$ there are valid states with $k$ free agents $o_0,\dots,o_{k-1}$ of the following form. Each $o_i$ is the root of a binary tree of depth $d$ whose agents want the goods of their two children, and the leaves of the tree of $o_{i+1}$ hold their tops and are blockers of $o_i$ (indices modulo $k$). Either each leaf has a leftover good of its own, or the leaves of each tree take the goods of a common pool of $k$ in turn, so that a tree's leaves have $\min(2^d,k)$ distinct leftover goods. In these states every cycle of want and pair arrows has exactly $k$ pair arrows, and no free agent can finish if and only if $k\le2^d$, the number of leaves of a tree. For $k=2$ and $d=1$ this is the instance of Appendix~\ref{sec:example}. We omit the proofs of these two facts; both are machine-checked in Lean for every $k$ and $d$.

\section{Limits of the Shape}\label{app:limits}

Could every instance have an \efxz{} allocation in which all bundles have at most two goods, or at least one in which all bundles have at most three? In both cases the answer is no. Write $\theta_i(B)$, the \emph{threat} of $B$ to $i$, for the largest value of $v_i(B\setminus\{h\})$ over $h\in B$, or $0$ if $B=\emptyset$. Agent $i$ does not envy $X_j$ in the sense of \efxz{} if and only if $v_i(X_i)\ge\theta_i(X_j)$, and $i$ is \emph{safe} if this holds for every $j\ne i$. By additivity, $\theta_i(B)=0$ if $|B|\le1$, $\theta_i(B)\le v_i(B\cap R_i)$ with equality if $B$ has a good outside $R_i$, and $\theta_i(\{g,h\})=\max(v_i(g),v_i(h))$.

\begin{lemma}[cases of safety]\label{lem:L5}
Let agent $i$ have $R_i=\{a,b,c\}$ with $v_i(a)>v_i(b)>v_i(c)>0$ and $v_i(a)<v_i(b)+v_i(c)$, and let $X$ be an allocation. Then $i$ is safe in $X$ if and only if one of the following holds:
\begin{description}
\item[(T)] $i$ holds $a$, and either $i$ also holds $b$ or $c$, or $b$ and $c$ are not together in a bundle of at least three goods;
\item[(BC)] $i$ holds $b$ and $c$;
\item[(B)] $i$ holds $b$, and $a$ is alone;
\item[(C)] $i$ holds $c$, and $a$ and $b$ are alone;
\item[(E)] $a$, $b$ and $c$ are all alone.
\end{description}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We also write $a$, $b$, $c$ for the values. The sums of subsets of $\{a,b,c\}$ are ordered as $0<c<b<a<b+c<a+c<a+b<a+b+c$. Let $S=X_i\cap R_i$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $S\supseteq\{a,b\}$ or $S\supseteq\{a,c\}$: every other bundle meets $R_i$ in at most one good, so its threat is at most $b<a+c$. This gives (T).
\item $S=\{b,c\}$: every threat is at most $a<b+c$. This gives (BC).
\item $S=\{a\}$: if $b$, $c$ are in different bundles, each threat is at most $b<a$; if $\{b,c\}$ is a bundle, its threat is $b<a$; if a bundle strictly contains $\{b,c\}$, it has a third good, outside $R_i$, and its threat is $b+c>a$. This gives (T).
\item $S=\{b\}$: if $a$ is not alone, its bundle has threat at least $a>b$; if $a$ is alone, every threat is at most $c<b$. This gives (B).
\item $S=\{c\}$: as before, $a$ must be alone, and if $b$ is not alone, its bundle has threat at least $b>c$. This gives (C).
\item $S=\emptyset$: a bundle with a good of $R_i$ and at least two goods has a positive threat. This gives (E).
\end{itemize}
Conversely, each of (T), (BC), (B), (C), (E) describes situations shown to be safe above. Case (T) corresponds to the first and third items, and (BC) to $S=\{b,c\}$ or $S=\{a,b,c\}$. Case (B) forces $a\notin S$, so $S=\{b\}$ with $a$ alone, or $S=\{b,c\}$; (C) forces $S=\{c\}$ with $a$ and $b$ alone; and (E) means that $S$ is empty or a single alone good, which is (T), (B) or (C). \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[limits of the shape]\label{prop:neg}
Take values with $v_i(a_i)>v_i(b_i)>v_i(c_i)>0$ and $v_i(a_i)<v_i(b_i)+v_i(c_i)$ for every agent.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] Let agents $0$, $1$, $2$ rank $g_0\succ g_1\succ p_i$, where $p_i$ is valued only by agent $i$. No \efxz{} allocation has all bundles of at most two goods, and $\{g_0\},\{g_1\},\{p_0,p_1,p_2\}$, to the three agents in any order, is \efxz{}.
\item[(b)] Let agents $0$, $1$, $2$, $3$ rank $g_0\succ g_2\succ p_0$, $g_0\succ g_2\succ p_1$, $g_1\succ g_2\succ p_2$ and $g_1\succ g_2\succ p_3$, with $p_i$ valued only by agent $i$. Every \efxz{} allocation has bundle sizes $4,1,1,1$, and one exists.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) Five goods in three bundles of at most two goods form bundles of sizes $2,2,1$, so exactly one good is alone. Two of the agents do not hold their top $g_0$; for them case (T) fails, and (C) and (E) need two or three alone goods. Hence both are in case (BC) or (B), and both hold $g_1$, which is impossible. In $\{g_0\},\{g_1\},\{p_0,p_1,p_2\}$ the agents are in cases (T), (B) and (C), respectively.

(b) For each of the tops $g_0$, $g_1$, some agent ranking it first does not hold it; call such an agent a \emph{loser}. The two losers differ. A loser is safe only by (BC) or (B), which need it to hold $g_2$, or by (C) or (E), which need $g_2$ alone. The two losers cannot both hold $g_2$, so $g_2$ is alone, and nobody is in case (BC). A loser holding $\{g_2\}$ needs its top alone, and a loser not holding $g_2$ needs its top and $g_2$ alone. Hence $g_0$, $g_1$, $g_2$ are alone, held by three agents, and the fourth bundle is $\{p_0,p_1,p_2,p_3\}$. Such an allocation exists: agents $0$, $1$, $2$, $3$ receive $g_0$, $g_2$, $g_1$ and the four goods $p_i$, respectively, and are in cases (T), (B), (T) and (C). \qed
\end{proof}

\noindent In (a) every agent values exactly three goods and is strictly balanced, so the one large bundle allowed by Theorem~\ref{thm:main} cannot be avoided; by (b), its size cannot be bounded by three either.

\paragraph{An instance that no earlier result covers.} Six agents value $\{g_0,g_1,g_6\}$, $\{g_0,g_1,g_7\}$, $\{g_0,g_2,g_8\}$, $\{g_2,g_3,g_4\}$, $\{g_3,g_5,g_9\}$ and $\{g_4,g_5,g_{10}\}$, with pairwise distinct values between $1$ and $2$. Then $n=6$ and $m=11$, and every agent values exactly three goods and is strictly balanced, so Theorem~\ref{thm:main} applies. The other results of Table~\ref{tab:rw} do not, as we check row by row:
\begin{itemize}
\item $n=3$~\cite{ChaudhuryGM20} and $n=4$~\cite{AlkassarFM26}: here $n=6$.
\item At most three distinct valuations~\cite{HVGNV25}: the six agents value different sets of goods, so the six valuations differ.
\item $m\le n+3$~\cite{Mahara21} and $m=n+4$~\cite{Bratby26}: here $m=n+5$.
\item At most two agents valuing each good~\cite{ChristodoulouFKS23,AfshinmehrAMMS26,SgouritsaS25,KavianiSS24} (a good is an edge between the agents that value it): three agents value $g_0$.
\item Hypergraphs of girth at least four~\cite{LianeasSS26}: the first two agents both value $g_0$ and $g_1$, a cycle of length two.
\item At most four relevant goods per agent~\cite{ViswanathanM24}: this result covers the instance, but for EFX rather than \efxz{}.
\item At most two relevant goods per agent~\cite{MrdEfx}: here three.
\end{itemize}
The script of Appendix~\ref{sec:evidence} checks these properties and runs \DE{} on $2{,}000$ random choices of the values.

\paragraph{Real values.} The model has natural-number values, and real values are reduced to it by an encoding. Let agent $i$ have relevant goods of values $x_1,x_2,x_3>0$. (With one or two relevant goods, the missing slots repeat the first good in these comparisons, so some compared sums count a good twice; no set of goods contains a missing slot.) After the goods common to both sides are cancelled, a comparison of two sums of its values becomes a comparison of two disjoint sets of these goods, and since the values are positive, it is decided by twelve basic comparisons: $x_k\le x_l$ for $k\ne l$, and $x_k\le x_l+x_r$ and $x_l+x_r\le x_k$ for $\{k,l,r\}=\{1,2,3\}$. Both directions are asked, so that ties are recorded. Using an oracle for $\le$, a program first asks the $m$ questions $v_i(g)\le0$, which find the relevant goods, and then these twelve questions. It gives the three goods the first of $31$ triples of natural numbers with the same twelve answers, and every other good keeps the value $0$; Lean's kernel checks that this table of triples covers every consistent pattern of answers. The new values $w$ answer every comparison of two sums of an agent's values as $v$ does (\file{EFX.K3.agree_surrogate}). This suffices, because the \efxz{} condition $v_i(X_i)\ge v_i(X_j\setminus\{h\})$ compares two such sums; hence an allocation is \efxz{} for $w$ if and only if it is \efxz{} for $v$, and the relevant goods are the same (\file{EFX.efx0_iff_of_agree}, \file{EFX.numRelevant_eq_of_agree}). Consequently, \DE{} run on $w$ returns an allocation that is \efxz{} for $v$, of the same shape and within the same bounds (\file{EFX.DE.deOrd_correct}).

\paragraph{Running time.} A counted program, proved to return exactly \DE{}'s output (\file{de_eq_spec}), takes at most $750(n+1)(n+m+1)$ units, including reading the input (\file{deC_cost}). As in a random-access machine, each elementary operation, comparison, array read and array write costs one unit, and arrays are used single-threaded; a natural number costs one unit whatever its size. On real values, the encoding adds at most $n(m+12)$ comparisons and $10nm+971n$ other units (\file{EFX.K3.surrogateC_cost}, \file{deOrdC_cost}).

\paragraph{What is not formalized.} (1)~That the units match the steps of a machine, a property of the counted program checked by reading it. (2)~That the nonnegative reals satisfy the axioms of Lean's class of values. Core Lean has no real numbers, so this textbook fact is assumed (the instances for natural numbers and integers are checked). (3)~That \DE{} run directly on real values makes the same decisions as on the encoded values. This is true because every decision is a comparison that the encoding preserves, but Lean states the theorem for \DE{} run on $w$. (4)~That the Lean statements say what the text says. They were written in AI agent sessions of Claude Code, the coding assistant that helped with this work, and compared with the text by independent AI sessions (\file{k3/simplify/po/referee/lean_audit*.md} in~\cite{Repo}), not by a human; the model and the statement of \file{de_correct} are short enough to check by hand. (5)~The second proof mentioned below, the novelty claim, and the tests.

\paragraph{Tests.} The tests are evidence, not proof. Three implementations, each asserting every claim of a proof and checking its outputs against the raw definition of \efxz{} in exact arithmetic, ran without failure on every profile with $n=2$ and $m\le6$ or with $n=3$ and $m\le7$, on part of the profiles with $n=4$ and $m\le6$, on core instances with $n=5,6$, and on random profiles with up to $9$ agents. The counts are about $1.7$ million valid states for the proof of Section~\ref{sec:improve}, about $15.5$ million for an independently found second proof (a walk instead of a choice of distinct leftover goods), and about $2.9$ million runs of a referee's implementation written from the proof notes alone (\file{k3/simplify/po/} in~\cite{Repo}). The first and third implementations run \DE{}'s loop with the same three steps (up to the choice among leftover goods); the second tests the lemmas of Sections~\ref{sec:finish} and~\ref{sec:improve} on its own loop, which lets a free agent holding nothing finish first. The script \file{paper/k3-simple/examples/check_examples.py}, written from this paper with the loop as stated here, recomputes every example and every computed number in the paper and runs \DE{}, peeling included, on random instances with ties, unbalanced agents and goods that nobody values.
